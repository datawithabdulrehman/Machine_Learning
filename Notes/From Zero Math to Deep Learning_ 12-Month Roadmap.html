From Zero Math to Deep Learning

A 12-month plan at about 8-10 hours a week. You do not need to be a "math person." Deep learning uses a small slice of math, and almost all of it can be understood visually before you meet a formula. If a phase takes longer, that is fine: this is a staircase, not a race.

Foundations

Weeks 1-6 · Python + NumPy + the math you forgot

Core math concepts

  • Weeks 1-2: fractions, percentages, exponents, square roots, and logarithms (the opposite of exponents).
  • Weeks 3-4: solving for x, functions as machines (input in, output out), reading graphs, straight lines (y = mx + b), and the sum symbol Σ ("add them all up").
  • Weeks 5-6: Python basics (variables, loops, functions, lists, dictionaries), then NumPy arrays, shapes, indexing, broadcasting.

AI/ML connection

A neural network is a giant function: numbers in, numbers out. Algebra teaches you to read it, and logs and exponents appear in nearly every loss function. This phase is learning the alphabet before writing sentences.

Code application

Python, NumPy (the base of every AI library), and Matplotlib for plotting your functions.

Best free visual resources

  • Khan Academy: Pre-algebra and Algebra 1 (use the quizzes to skip what you know)
  • Desmos.com: type y = 2x + 1 and see it
  • Corey Schafer's Python tutorials or freeCodeCamp's Python course
  • Jay Alammar: "A Visual Intro to NumPy"

Milestone project

  • Plot 5 functions (line, parabola, exponential, log, sine) with Matplotlib.
  • Write a mean function with plain Python, then redo it in one line with NumPy.
  • Time a Python loop against a NumPy operation on 1 million numbers (this is vectorization).

Linear algebra for AI

Weeks 7-12 · Vectors, matrices, dot products, eigenvalues

Core math concepts

  • Weeks 7-8: vectors (a list of numbers and an arrow in space), adding and scaling, the dot product, vector length.
  • Weeks 9-10: matrices, matrix multiplication, transpose, identity, matrices as transformations that stretch, rotate, and squish space.
  • Weeks 11-12: determinant (how much space gets scaled), inverse, eigenvectors and eigenvalues, and a gentle first look at PCA.

AI/ML connection

  • Vectors describe everything. A photo, a word, or a customer becomes a list of numbers, and similar things get similar lists.
  • The dot product measures similarity. It is the math behind how Transformers decide which words matter to each other.
  • Every neural network layer is a matrix multiplication. Learning means adjusting the numbers inside those matrices.
  • Eigenvectors are directions that don't turn under a transformation: the spine of your data. PCA uses them to find what matters most.

Analogy: data is a crumpled map, and matrices are the hands that stretch and flatten it until the pattern shows.

Code application

NumPy's np.dot, @, np.linalg.inv, np.linalg.eig, np.linalg.svd, and scikit-learn's PCA.

Best free visual resources

  • 3Blue1Brown: "Essence of Linear Algebra" (watch this first, slowly)
  • Immersive Linear Algebra (immersivemath.com), an interactive book
  • Khan Academy: Linear Algebra, for practice
  • Gilbert Strang's MIT 18.06 lectures, optional later

Milestone project

  • Implement matrix multiplication from scratch with loops and check it against NumPy.
  • Build a tiny movie recommender: 5 users and 5 movies as vectors, using cosine similarity to find who is most alike.
  • Run PCA on the Iris dataset, squash 4 dimensions to 2, and plot it.

Calculus for training

Weeks 13-18 · Derivatives, partial derivatives, the chain rule

Core math concepts

  • Weeks 13-14: how functions change, limits (intuition only), the derivative as slope or speed of change.
  • Weeks 15-16: derivative rules (power, product, chain), derivatives of eˣ, ln(x), sin, cos, and second derivatives (curvature).
  • Weeks 17-18: partial derivatives (change one input, freeze the rest), the gradient, and a deep dive on the chain rule, the most important idea here.

AI/ML connection

Training asks one question: "If I nudge this number inside the model, does the error go up or down, and by how much?" That is a derivative.

  • The derivative is a speedometer for how fast the error changes.
  • The gradient is a compass pointing uphill. Walk the opposite way to lower the error.
  • The chain rule is connected gears. A network is many functions stacked together, and the chain rule tells you how turning the first gear moves the last. This is how backpropagation works.

Code application

SymPy for symbolic derivatives, NumPy for numerical ones (nudge x a tiny bit and measure), and a first look at PyTorch autograd (.backward()) confirming your hand calculations.

Best free visual resources

  • 3Blue1Brown: "Essence of Calculus"
  • Khan Academy: Differential Calculus, plus the partial derivatives and gradient parts of Multivariable Calculus
  • Professor Leonard (YouTube) for slower, classroom-style lessons
  • Desmos: draw a curve and drag a tangent line along it

Milestone project

  • Write a function that approximates the derivative of any function numerically and compare it to the exact answer.
  • Plot f(x) = x² with tangent lines at several points.
  • Find the gradient of f(x, y) = x² + 3y² by hand, then verify it with PyTorch autograd.
  • Differentiate a chain like f(g(h(x))) by hand and confirm it numerically.

Probability & data statistics

Weeks 19-25 · Mean and variance, distributions, Bayes' theorem

Core math concepts

  • Weeks 19-20: basic probability, "and"/"or" rules, conditional probability, independence.
  • Week 21: Bayes' theorem (updating beliefs with evidence).
  • Weeks 22-23: random variables, mean, variance, standard deviation, and key distributions (Uniform, Bernoulli, Binomial, Normal).
  • Weeks 24-25: the Central Limit Theorem (intuition), correlation vs causation, sampling, maximum likelihood ("which settings make my data least surprising?"), entropy and cross-entropy.

AI/ML connection

The real world is noisy, so models output probabilities, not certainties: not "this is a cat" but "92% cat."

  • Bayes' theorem is the detective's rule: start with a hunch, find a clue, update your belief.
  • Distributions describe what "normal" looks like, and are used for weight initialization, noise, and uncertainty.
  • Cross-entropy, the most common classification loss, measures how surprised the model is by the right answer. Less surprise means a better model.

Code application

Pandas for data, SciPy.stats for distributions, Seaborn and Matplotlib for plots, and np.random for simulations.

Best free visual resources

  • StatQuest with Josh Starmer: "Statistics Fundamentals," plus his Bayes' Theorem, Normal Distribution, and Cross-Entropy videos
  • Seeing Theory (seeing-theory.brown.edu), interactive probability visuals
  • Khan Academy: Statistics and Probability
  • 3Blue1Brown: the Bayes' theorem and Central Limit Theorem videos

Milestone project

  • Simulate 10,000 dice rolls, coin flips, and sample averages, and watch the bell curve appear.
  • Build a Naive Bayes spam filter from scratch on a small text dataset.
  • Explore a real dataset (mean, variance, histograms, correlations) and write a half-page summary of what you found.

Optimization & algorithms

Weeks 26-31 · Cost functions, gradient descent, backpropagation

Core math concepts

  • Weeks 26-27: loss functions (MSE, cross-entropy): "wrongness" as a single number.
  • Weeks 28-29: gradient descent, learning rate, stochastic and mini-batch versions, the ideas behind momentum and Adam.
  • Week 30: overfitting vs underfitting, regularization (L1/L2), train/validation/test splits.
  • Week 31: backpropagation and computational graphs.

AI/ML connection

Everything you have learned comes together here.

  • Loss function: a score for how wrong the model is.
  • Gradient descent: walking downhill in thick fog. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope, so you step downhill and repeat. The learning rate is your step size: too big and you overshoot, too small and it takes forever.
  • Backpropagation: tracing blame in a factory. When the final product is defective, you walk backward through every station and ask how much each contributed. The chain rule assigns the blame.

Code application

Pure NumPy first, so nothing is hidden. Then scikit-learn to compare against the official linear and logistic regression.

Best free visual resources

  • StatQuest: "Gradient Descent, Step-by-Step," the Neural Networks series, and "Backpropagation Main Ideas"
  • 3Blue1Brown: Neural Networks series, chapters 1-4
  • Andrej Karpathy: "Neural Networks: Zero to Hero," starting with the micrograd video (watch once for the story, once while coding along)
  • TensorFlow Playground (playground.tensorflow.org): watch a network train and the loss fall

Milestone project

  • Linear regression with gradient descent from scratch in NumPy, plotting the loss falling.
  • Logistic regression from scratch for a yes/no problem.
  • A 2-layer neural network in pure NumPy (forward pass, loss, backprop, update) that learns XOR or digits.
  • Follow along with micrograd so you have seen backprop built by hand once.

Transition to deep learning

Weeks 32-52 · Neural networks, CNNs, RNNs, Transformers

Core math concepts

Nothing brand new, only your old tools in new outfits: matrix multiplication in every layer, dot products as attention scores, the chain rule through deeper networks, softmax and cross-entropy for probabilities, and Adam for training.

A. PyTorch + neural networks (weeks 32-36)Tensors, autograd, nn.Module, training loops, DataLoaders, activations (ReLU, sigmoid, softmax), dropout, batch norm, free GPUs on Colab and Kaggle. Milestone: MNIST classifier at 97%+ accuracy.
B. CNNs for images (weeks 37-41)Convolutions, filters, pooling, LeNet and ResNet, transfer learning. Milestone: CIFAR-10 or cats-vs-dogs classifier using a pretrained ResNet.
C. RNNs and LSTMs for sequences (weeks 42-45)Hidden state, vanishing gradients, LSTM/GRU. Milestone: a character-level text generator.
D. Attention and Transformers (weeks 46-50)Attention, self-attention, positional encoding, encoder/decoder, how GPT-style models work. Milestone: follow Karpathy's "Let's build GPT" or fine-tune a small Hugging Face model on your own task.
Capstone (weeks 51-52)One project you care about, end to end: data, model, evaluation, a clean GitHub README, and a short write-up.

AI/ML connection

  • CNNs: a filter is a small magnifying glass sliding over an image, hunting one pattern (an edge, then a curve, then an eye, then a face).
  • RNNs: reading a sentence word by word while carrying a memory note forward.
  • Transformers: every word looks at every other word at once and decides who is relevant. That is attention, and it is just dot products plus softmax, which you already know.

Code application

PyTorch (primary), torchvision, Hugging Face Transformers, and Matplotlib or TensorBoard for tracking training.

Best free visual resources

  • 3Blue1Brown: Neural Networks chapters 5-7 (Transformers and attention)
  • StatQuest: CNN, RNN/LSTM, Attention, and Transformer videos
  • CNN Explainer (poloclub.github.io/cnn-explainer)
  • Jay Alammar: "The Illustrated Transformer," and Chris Olah: "Understanding LSTM Networks"
  • fast.ai: Practical Deep Learning, plus Stanford CS231n and Karpathy's Zero to Hero for depth

Milestone project

Your capstone: a project you care about, published with a clear README, such as image classification on a topic you love, text classification, or fine-tuning a language model.

Tips that make the difference

  1. Intuition first, formulas second. Watch the visual explanation, then meet the formula. It will already make sense.
  2. Code every concept. If you can build it from scratch, you understand it.
  3. Never skip the milestone. It shows what you actually know versus what only felt familiar while watching.
  4. Keep a "stuck log." Write down what confused you and revisit it in two weeks. Half of it will have resolved itself.
  5. Put every milestone on GitHub with a short README. By week 52 you will have a portfolio that shows your growth.
  6. Practice on Kaggle once you reach Phase 4 or 5, and see how others approach problems.
  7. Be kind to yourself. Feeling lost on a new topic means you are learning. Go back one video, try again, and move on.

Weekly rhythm for every phase: 3 sessions watching and reading (pause to predict what comes next), 2 sessions coding the idea yourself, 1 session of practice problems plus a short note explaining it in your own words.
